Water Mitigation Process in Los Banos: What Happens Next

Mitigation is the part of a water loss that stops the damage from growing. This is the order it follows.

By the Los Banos Water Damage Pros team. Updated November 10, 2026.

(831) 271-0459
Call Now

Water mitigation means taking action to stop damage from growing. It is different from repair. Mitigation removes water, dries the structure and protects what can be saved. Repair rebuilds what could not be saved. Most of what you pay for early in a loss is mitigation, and it sets up everything that follows.

This post walks through what to expect after you call in Los Banos, from the questions the dispatcher asks to the readings that tell the crew they are done.

Step 1: The call and the first questions

The dispatcher asks where the water is coming from, how long it has been running, how deep it is and whether anything electrical is wet. Those answers decide the crew, the equipment and the safety steps. If the water is still running, you may be asked to close the main valve while you wait.

The line is open 24 hours a day. A crew is sent as soon as one is available, and the dispatcher tells you honestly what that looks like.

Step 2: Safety check and source control

On arrival the crew confirms the water is off and checks for electrical hazards. They identify the water category under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard. Category 1 comes from a clean source, Category 2 carries some contamination and Category 3 is grossly contaminated, such as sewage or floodwater.

The category changes the plan. Clean water can often be dried, while contaminated water means porous materials are removed.

Step 3: Extraction

Standing water is removed with pumps and extraction units. Taking out the bulk of the water first is the fastest way to reduce drying time. This step is covered in more detail on the water extraction page.

Step 4: Moisture mapping

Water travels. It moves under baseboards, into wall bottoms and under flooring. The crew uses moisture meters and a thermal camera to find where it went, and marks the wet areas. Those readings become the baseline.

Step 5: Removal of what cannot dry

Some materials are not worth drying. Saturated carpet pad, wet insulation and soft drywall are common removals, and anything touched by sewage or floodwater is discarded. Removing them early gives the structure air and speeds up drying.

Step 6: Drying equipment and monitoring

Air moversPush air across wet surfacesWhen surface readings reach a dry level
DehumidifiersPull moisture from the airWhen the room air and materials are dry
Moisture metersTrack progress each visitUsed until the final reading
Thermal cameraShows hidden wet areasUsed at mapping and the final check

The crew returns each day or two to read the meters and adjust equipment. Ending early is the common mistake, because a floor that feels dry may still hold moisture below. According to the EPA, water-damaged areas and items should be dried within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth, and that target guides the first days.

Step 7: Documentation and the handoff to repair

When readings match a dry reference, the equipment is removed. You receive the readings and photos, which your insurer may ask for. Repairs, such as new drywall, flooring and paint, can then be planned on a dry structure.

What you can do during mitigation

Keep the equipment running and do not unplug it to charge a phone or run a vacuum. Keep doors and windows closed in the drying area unless the crew tells you otherwise, since open doors let in humid air. Keep pets and children away from the equipment, and tell the crew about anything that seems wrong, such as a new smell or a tripped breaker.

Take your own photos each day. They show progress, and they help if a question comes up later. Save any paperwork the crew gives you, including daily readings, because insurers often ask for them.

How long each phase usually lasts

These are general ranges. A small clean-water leak in one room moves quickly. A multi-room loss or a slab that absorbed water moves slowly, and the readings, not the clock, decide the end.

Questions to ask the crew on day one

  • What is the water category, and how did you decide?
  • Which materials are you removing, and why?
  • How many pieces of equipment are running, and when will you return?
  • What readings tell you the structure is dry?
  • What paperwork will I receive at the end?

A crew that welcomes these questions is a crew that can show its work. Write the answers down, and share them with your insurer if you file a claim.

Mitigation in Los Banos

Los Banos winters bring storms that can push water into homes, and summers bring long cooling seasons that load condensate lines. Mitigation timing differs by season and cause, so tell the dispatcher what happened and when. The more specific you are, the better the first visit goes.

If you manage a rental or a business, tell the property owner at the same time you call. Access, keys and insurance contacts are easier to line up on the first day.

Common questions

How long does mitigation take?

Extraction can take hours, and drying usually takes days. Readings decide when it is done, not a calendar.

Is mitigation the same as repair?

No. Mitigation stops damage and dries the structure. Repair rebuilds what could not be saved.

Do I need to be home during drying?

Not for the whole time, but equipment needs power and access, and the crew needs to return to take readings. Leave the equipment running as instructed.
